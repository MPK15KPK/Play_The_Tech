# CONTENT-GUIDE.md

How to write a comparison. Short, because most of the rules are the same
five things repeated.

---

## The shape

```
Title:    {Tool A} vs {Tool B} for {who it's for}
Summary:  40–60 words. The answer, outright. No preamble.
Table:    8–12 rows, commercial rows first
Body:     pros/cons, 2–3 real differences, verdict, FAQ
Sources:  the two vendor links
```

---

## Summary — the most important 60 words on the page

It sits under the H1 and it is the passage most likely to get lifted into an
AI answer or a snippet. Answer the question in the first sentence, then add
context.

Bad: "Choosing between Notion AI and Copilot depends on several factors
that we'll explore below."

Good: "Copilot is the better fit if your team already runs Microsoft 365 —
it works inside Word and Excel with no extra licence. Notion AI is stronger
for teams that keep documentation in Notion and want AI in the same place."

---

## Table

Row order — commercial first, capability second, support last:

```
Price from
Free trial
Best for
Free plan
{capability rows}
API access
Support
```

Rules:
- Every price gets a currency and a period: `$139.95/mo`
- Unknown is "Not published", never blank and never a dash
- Numbers align in columns (tabular figures)
- Mark the winner per row — with a symbol, not colour alone
- Every figure comes from the vendor's own pricing page

---

## Body

**Pros and cons per tool.** 5–6 pros, 4–5 cons. If the cons list reads soft,
the article isn't credible and readers can tell instantly. Every tool has a
cost, a limit, and a wrong buyer.

**"Skip it if" for each tool.** One line. This is the highest-trust sentence
in any comparison and almost nobody writes it.

**Two or three real differences.** Skip anything where the tools are
equivalent — shared features help nobody choose. Lead each section with the
conclusion, then justify it.

**Verdict.** Mandatory, and it must be specific:

> **Choose A if** you're under 20 seats and already on Microsoft 365.
> **Choose B if** your docs live in Notion and you need AI in the same place.

"It depends on your needs" is a failed article. It always depends on needs —
name them and answer for each.

**FAQ.** 3–5 real questions, 2–4 sentences each. Take them from what people
actually search, not what's convenient to answer. Q&A is the single most
cited content format there is.

---

## Writing rules

- **Question-shaped headings.** "How do they compare?" beats "Feature
  comparison" — question headings match what people ask AI engines.
- **Answer first, always.** Each section opens with the conclusion. 44% of
  AI citations come from the first third of a page.
- **Every section stands alone.** No "as mentioned above", no unresolved
  pronouns, no opening with "However". Retrieval systems chunk pages; an
  unmoored chunk is unusable.
- **Specific beats clever.** Not "powerful reporting" but "exports to Excel;
  no scheduled email delivery below $400/mo".
- **Vary sentence length.** Uniform 18-word sentences read as machine-written.
- **Never write:** "In today's fast-paced business environment", "It's worth
  noting that", "Let's dive in", "isn't just X, it's Y", "In conclusion".

---

## Where facts come from

**The vendor's own pricing page and documentation.** Open it, read it, link
to it.

Not from other comparison sites. Aside from the credibility problem, Google's
spam policy names "stitching or combining content from different web pages
without adding value" — and blending the top few results is exactly the
pattern the March 2026 update demoted.

Use search to find out *which* tools exist and *what* buyers ask. Then close
those tabs and write from primary sources.

**The test before publishing:** does this article contain at least one fact
that isn't on any of the pages you read? A price you checked yourself, a
limit you found in the docs, a "skip it if" nobody else wrote. If not, a
reader could just read those pages instead — and search engines reach the
same conclusion.

---

## Don't publish a post you can't say anything real on

If you can't name a genuine difference between the two tools, skip that
comparison. A gap in coverage costs nothing. A run of thin posts is what
gets a whole site demoted, and it's the most common way sites like this die.

---

## Monthly cadence

4–6 posts a month is realistic and sustainable. One category at a time.

Fifty good posts beat five hundred thin ones — that's not encouragement,
it's how the ranking systems actually work.

---

## Every quarter

Re-check prices on published posts and update them. Pages not refreshed
quarterly are roughly 3x more likely to lose their AI citations entirely,
and a stale price you published becomes your accuracy problem once an engine
starts quoting it.

Editing a post updates `updated_at` automatically, which is what the
"Last updated" line shows.
